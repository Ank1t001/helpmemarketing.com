5What this suggests

What the reviewed posts suggest, with a test for each.

  1. What I observed
    6 of Venn’s 9 posts are customer stories that name a problem and a result, such as reimbursement time.
    My reading
    The account leans on customer proof more than product claims. One story repeats the 1% cashback rate without naming a plan.
    A possible test
    When a story mentions a rate, name the plan it applies to, and track rewards questions from new accounts (see test 1 in Lessons).
  2. What I observed
    14 of Airwallex’s 23 posts are partnerships or company events, and 4 of 23 name a specific product.
    My reading
    The global account builds an association with sport, founders and events more than it explains products. Public posts cannot show whether that works for Airwallex.
    A possible test
    In sponsorship posts, add one line naming a product and the markets it serves, then compare profile link clicks with similar posts that do not, using the account’s own analytics.
  3. What I observed
    Within Airwallex, partnership posts had the highest median likes + comments (237 from 6), but the median falls to 75 from 4 without collaborations.
    My reading
    Co-authors’ audiences and post age may explain more of the difference than the theme. Public counts cannot separate them.
    A possible test
    Read counts at the same age after posting and keep collaborations apart before comparing themes.

These are readings of public posts, not causes. The website tests are in Lessons.

5What this suggests

What the ads suggest, with a test for each.

  1. What I observed
    None of Venn’s, Float’s or Airwallex’s ad text mentions terms or conditions, including beside reward, interest and FX-fee claims.
    My reading
    An ad is often the first place a buyer meets the offer. Without the condition there, the website has to correct the impression later.
    A possible test
    Add the main condition to the ad text or the first screen the ad opens, and compare qualified sign-ups per click (see test 1 in Lessons).
  2. What I observed
    25 of Ramp’s 47 ads send people to pages for one industry: nonprofits, healthcare or construction.
    My reading
    Matching the ad and the page to one kind of business keeps the message consistent after the click.
    A possible test
    For your largest audience, compare a matched landing page with a general one on qualified sign-ups per click, using your own analytics.
  3. What I observed
    102 of the 119 ads started in the 30 days before collection; one Loop ad had run since June and two Venn ads since July.
    My reading
    A one-day snapshot shows what runs now, not a long-term strategy. Running a long time does not prove an ad works.
    A possible test
    Repeat the collection monthly and track which messages keep running alongside your own results.

Lessons

Three changes to test on your own website. These are proposals, not proven results.

  1. Proposed test, not a result

    Test 1. Put the condition beside the reward

    What I noticed
    2 of 5 platforms that published a reward rate. Venn and Loop published reward statements that did not agree for some plans. On each site, the rate and the condition that limits it sat on different pages.
    Change to try
    Show the spending threshold, cap or plan restriction beside the reward rate.
    What to measure
    Qualified applications per page visitor, alongside reward-related questions.
  2. Proposed test, not a result

    Test 2. Explain what “no FX fee” covers

    What I noticed
    6 of 6 platforms limited the no-FX-fee claim to a specific situation. Each of the six reviewed no-FX-fee claims applied to a particular situation, such as spending from a balance in the same currency. Banking and card transactions can differ: Keep’s pricing lists a 0% FX fee for banking, while its card agreement sets a 3% card conversion fee.
    Change to try
    Name the transaction covered and explain what applies otherwise.
    What to measure
    Completed applications per page visitor, alongside FX questions and disputed charges.
  3. Proposed test, not a result

    Test 3. Explain the next step before the form

    What I noticed
    1 of 6 first screens listed the steps ahead. Only Loop’s reviewed first screen listed later steps. The routes served different purposes, including account sign-ups, a card application and a demo request, and Keep’s reviewed route was closed.

Finding 1. Reward rates and their conditions were often stated in different places

Test 1. Put the condition beside the reward Proposed test, not a result

Observed evidence
Venn's cards page says its cashback has no tiers, thresholds or caps, while its pricing footnote limits the eligible card spend on Essentials and Plus. Loop's cards page says rewards come on all spend, while its earn-rate table lists USD, EUR and GBP spend as not eligible on Basic. (O-VEN-04, O-LOP-04)
Customer question
Will I earn the advertised rate on my plan and at my spend level?
My interpretation
When the rate and its condition sit on different pages, a reader of either page sees only part of the offer.
Testable hypothesis
With the condition beside the rate, readers can check it before applying, so the version with the condition will produce at least as many qualified applications per card-page visitor, with fewer rewards questions from new customers.
One change
Show the cap, threshold or plan condition in the same block as the headline rate on the card page.
What stays comparable
The rate, the terms, the design and the button stay the same. Split card-page traffic at random, with the same traffic sources, the same attribution window and the same definition of a qualified application in both versions.
Primary measurement
Qualified applications (meeting your eligibility rules) per visitor to the card page. Count button clicks separately.
Guardrail
Rewards questions to sales and support in the first 30 days after sign-up, and plan mix.
When the result would be inconclusive
Inconclusive if rewards are not a main reason buyers choose you, if traffic is too small to separate the two versions, or if pricing or rewards change during the test. Legal review of the wording comes first.

Test 2. Explain what “no FX fee” covers Proposed test, not a result

Observed evidence
All six platforms published a no-FX-fee statement that covered a specific case, such as a matching-currency balance, four currencies or a USD card, and each site or its terms prices other conversions elsewhere. A general line answers that question only for the case it covers; the reader has to find the other cases in pricing or the terms.
Testable hypothesis
A line that names the covered transaction, followed by one line on what applies otherwise, will produce at least as many completed applications from the FX or cards page, with fewer FX questions and disputed charges after sign-up.

Replace a general "no FX fees" line with one that names the case it covers, followed by one line saying what applies otherwise.

The page layout, offer, fees, terms and button stay the same. Split traffic at random with the same audiences, the same visitor definition and the same attribution window. Completed applications per visitor to the FX or cards page. Report the application count and the visitor count as well as the rate, with the same visitor definition and attribution window in both versions.

FX-related support questions and disputed charges in the first 60 days after sign-up.
When the result would be inconclusive
Inconclusive if seasonal travel or trade cycles swamp the difference, or if fees change during the test. Required disclosures stay in place; this changes the summary, not the terms.

Status definitions and reconciliation

What each status means

Published
An applicable official source states it. This does not mean it was tested.
Not found in reviewed sources
The pages I reviewed did not establish it. It may exist elsewhere.
Not checked
No valid check was completed.
Conflicting
Relevant sources differ and the difference is unresolved.
Explicitly unavailable
An applicable source says it is not available.

A label beside an entry means it is not a plain published fact. “Published pages disagree” is the status Conflicting. A restriction set in bold (such as “Not offered to businesses in Quebec”) is the status Explicitly unavailable. “Not checked” means no valid check was completed, and “Not found in reviewed sources” means the reviewed pages did not establish it; neither means a feature is unavailable. Restriction lines beside a provider repeat what its pages state and are not a complete eligibility assessment.

Where a label says what could not be confirmed, two published statements did not settle the point (reconciliation class D in the 23 October 2026 research). For Airwallex, a follow-up check on 30 October 2026 found that pricing lists up to 1% cash rebates on local CAD spend and links to promotion terms whose period ends on 31 December 2025 unless Airwallex notifies otherwise; no extension was found, so current eligibility needs confirmation.
