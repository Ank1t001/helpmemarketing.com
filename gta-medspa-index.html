What the data says

  1. Posting more does almost nothing. Across 176 clinics, how often you post has essentially no relationship to how many people see you. The statistical correlation is 0.06, which is another way of saying zero.
  2. The clinics that post least reach most. Forty-three clinics posted 358 times between them and reached a median of 1,272 people per reel. Forty-six others posted 1,024 times and reached 318.
  3. Half the category is invisible on video. Of 341 clinics with an Instagram account, only 176 cleared three reels in 90 days. The rest are paying for an account nobody is watching.
  4. Two in three clinics publish something a regulator could question. Most commonly a prescription drug named alongside what it treats, which Health Canada does not permit in consumer advertising.

Finding one

Posting more does almost nothing

This is the finding that should change how you spend your week. We expected cadence to predict reach. It does not.

We tested the relationship between how often a clinic posts and how many people see a typical reel. The correlation is 0.06. For comparison, a correlation of 1.0 would mean cadence perfectly predicts reach and 0 would mean the two are unrelated. At 0.06, they are unrelated.

Splitting the 176 clinics on both axes makes the cost visible. The four groups below are the same market, cut by how much they publish and how far it travels.

Read the two extremes together. The Grinding group published 1,024 posts in 90 days and reached a median of 318 people per reel. The Efficient group published 358 and reached 1,272. That is roughly one third of the output for four times the reach.

If you are in the Grinding group, the answer is not a fifth post this week. Those 46 clinics are already producing more than anyone else in the market and getting the least back for it. The constraint is what the content is, not how much of it there is.

Finding three

Where your clinic sits changes the benchmark

A Durham clinic performing at its regional median does roughly half what a Toronto clinic at its median does. If you are comparing yourself to a clinic you follow in another region, you are using the wrong yardstick.

The Index

The top 25

Ranked on the Index score: reach carries 70% and response carries 30%. Reach is the median number of plays on a clinic's reels. Response is comments per thousand plays delivered, which measures whether the people who saw it did anything.

Notice how many of the top 25 sit in the Efficient group. Thirteen of them posted ten times or fewer in 90 days. Position 8 posted five times. Position 10 posted four.

Finding four

Most of this market is advertising in a way Canadian regulators do not allow

We read all 2,793 captions from the ranked clinics against two rule sets. Two in three clinics published at least one post that would not survive a complaint.

The most common single issue is the first one, and it is the easiest to fix. A post that says Botox, 20 units, $99 is fine. Health Canada permits name, price and quantity. A post that says Botox for forehead lines is not, because it adds the condition being treated. Health Canada names Botox specifically as a product frequently marketed this way.

The harder one is before-and-after. Ontario's policy for physicians allows it with conditions, but a separate provision says it must not be displayed where members of the public are likely to see it unsolicited. A public Instagram feed is close to the definition of unsolicited display, and no disclaimer fixes that.

Which rules apply to you

Health Canada's restriction on prescription drug advertising applies to everyone, whoever owns the clinic. The testimonial, before-and-after and superlative rules come from the College of Physicians and Surgeons of Ontario and bind physicians and the clinics they direct. If your clinic is nurse-led or not physician-directed, those three sit outside your college's policy, though your own regulator may have its own. This is a summary of published policy and not legal advice.

One pattern is worth flagging. The clinics at the top of the Index lean hardest on the two riskiest categories. Leading-tier clinics name a prescription drug with a claim on 13.4% of posts against 4.1% for the field, and post before-and-after on 13.4% against 4.0%. We are not claiming the risk is what buys the reach. Overall compliance rates barely differ by tier. But the content winning attention in this market is disproportionately the content carrying regulatory exposure, and that is worth knowing before you copy it.

The read

What the index says to do

Four moves, each drawn from a finding above. None of them is "post more".

If you are Grinding

Cut output, change the content

The 46 clinics in this group already publish more than anyone else and reach the least. Halve the calendar and spend the freed hours on the one reel a week that is built to travel. The Efficient group proves a third of the output can earn four times the reach.

If you are in the Field

Aim at Rising, not Leading

Field to Rising is 769 to about 2,057 plays per reel. Several Rising clinics post fewer than ten times in 90 days, so the gap is content quality, not effort. Leading, at 3,616, is a different game and not the next step.

Whatever your region

Benchmark against your own region first

A Durham clinic at 421 is at its regional median. A Toronto clinic at 421 is below it. Use the regional figure to judge yourself, and the 5,700-play ceiling that exists in every region to know the limit is not where you are.

Before you copy the leaders

Fix the drug-name captions this week

The most common exposure is a prescription drug named alongside what it treats, on 32% of ranked clinics. It is also the easiest fix: name, price and quantity are allowed, the condition is not. Before-and-after content needs a harder conversation with your regulator's policy.

Method

How we built this

Area
City of Toronto plus Durham, Halton, Peel and York regions. Collected municipality by municipality rather than by radius, because a radius from downtown reaches Hamilton while cutting off Oshawa, Clarington and Georgina.
Frame
786 business listings collected across 37 searches, reduced to 341 unique clinic Instagram accounts after removing day spas, salons, massage and physiotherapy, duplicate locations on one account, and broken links.
Window
90 days to 10 September 2026. Up to 30 posts per account, 2,810 posts across the ranked clinics.
Qualifying
Three or more reels in the window. Below that a median is built on too little to mean anything, so those 42 clinics appear in the data but are not ranked.
Reach
Median plays per reel. Not follower-adjusted: reel distribution is driven by recommendation rather than by who follows you, so dividing by followers would measure audience size instead of performance.
Response
Comments per thousand plays delivered, capped at the 95th percentile so a single giveaway post cannot distort a clinic's score.
Likes
Recorded but never scored. Thirty clinics hide their like counts, and the scraping tools in common use invent a value of 3 when likes are hidden rather than reporting them as unavailable.
Compliance
Caption text matched against published Health Canada and CPSO provisions, then a sample of every matched pattern was read by hand to remove false positives. Image content was not assessed, so before-and-after figures count only posts that describe themselves that way in the caption. The real figure is higher.

Questions

Common questions about this data

What is a good number of views for a medspa reel in the GTA?

The median GTA medical spa reel reaches 769 people. The top 25% of clinics reach 2,000 or more, and the top 10% reach 3,616 or more. Anything under 250 puts a clinic in the bottom 14% of the market.

Does posting more often grow a medspa Instagram account?

Not on its own. Across 176 GTA clinics the correlation between how often a clinic posts and how far its reels travel is 0.06, which is effectively zero. Forty-six clinics published 1,024 posts in 90 days and still reached a below-median 318 people per reel.

How many followers does a medspa need on Instagram?

Fewer than most owners assume. Instagram distributes reels through recommendation rather than through your follower list, so reach and follower count have come apart. This Index deliberately measures plays rather than followers for that reason.

Can a Canadian clinic advertise Botox on Instagram?

Only within limits. Health Canada restricts consumer advertising of a prescription drug to its name, price and quantity. Posting "Botox, 20 units, $99" is permitted. Adding what it treats, such as forehead lines or jaw tension, is not.

Can a medspa post before-and-after photos in Ontario?

Conditionally. The CPSO permits them when they show real patients, a typical outcome, and a disclaimer that results are not guaranteed. A separate provision says they must not be displayed where the public sees them unsolicited, which puts a public Instagram feed in question.
